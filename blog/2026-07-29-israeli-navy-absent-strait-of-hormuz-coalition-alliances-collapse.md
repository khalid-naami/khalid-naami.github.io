# Absent Fleet: Why Israel’s Navy Shuns the Strait of Hormuz

Despite open appeals from the U.S. administration for regional and international naval contributions to secure the Strait of Hormuz, the Israeli Navy has remained absent from the Gulf. This strategic absence highlights the limits of U.S.-Israeli coordination and the operational realities facing the Israeli fleet. With its maritime alliances in Europe and Asia fragmented, the U.S. Navy is left isolated, bearing the entire burden of operations against Iranian anti-ship networks.

## The Missing Fleet: Why Israel’s Navy Shuns the Strait

The absence of Israeli warships from the Strait of Hormuz has surprised observers who expected a unified U.S.-Israeli front. However, Israeli military planners recognize that deploying naval assets to the Gulf is tactically high-risk.

If Israeli vessels were integrated directly under U.S. command in the Persian Gulf, they would become immediate targets for Iranian anti-ship cruise missiles and suicide drone swarms. Given the size of the Israeli Navy, the loss of even a few Sa'ar 5 or Sa'ar 6 corvettes would degrade Israel's maritime defense, leaving its gas rigs in the Mediterranean vulnerable.

---

## The Houthi Obstacle at Bab al-Mandab

For Israeli warships to reach the Strait of Hormuz from their home ports, they must transit the Red Sea and pass through the Bab al-Mandab Strait. This narrow choke point is controlled by Houthi forces in Yemen.

The Houthis have maintained a targeting list for Israeli-affiliated shipping. Any deployment of Israeli corvettes through this sector would guarantee attacks by anti-ship ballistic missiles and sea-skimming drones. The high probability of damage during transit makes the deployment to the Gulf logistically unviable.

---

## Collapse of the Greece-Cyprus-Israel Trilateral Alliance

Israel’s regional maritime security strategy has been impacted by the collapse of the trilateral alliance between Israel, Greece, and Cyprus. A single Iranian drone strike targeting the British sovereign bases in Cyprus served as a warning to Eastern Mediterranean nations.

Greece and Cyprus have refused to participate in the Gulf naval coalition. Recognizing the threat of retaliatory strikes on their own shipping, Athens decided to maintain neutrality. Israel quietly supported Greece's decision, choosing to keep its Mediterranean partners out of the conflict to secure its western trade routes.

---

## The Failure of the Asian NATO Initiative

The U.S. administration's efforts to recruit Asian partners to secure the Strait of Hormuz have also failed. The concept of an "Asian NATO" has collapsed due to regional reluctance:
*   **Japan**: Tokyo has refused to send naval vessels to join the U.S.-led coalition, choosing to protect its commercial shipping through diplomatic neutrality.
*   **Australia**: Isolated from its traditional regional partners, Canberra has shown hesitation, leaving the U.S. Navy to operate without significant support from the Indo-Pacific.

This reluctance shows that Asian nations are unwilling to risk their energy security by engaging in a direct military confrontation with Iran.

---

## Internationalizing the Conflict: Trump’s Diplomatic Push

To offset the isolation of U.S. forces, President Donald Trump has attempted to internationalize the conflict, turning a bilateral U.S.-Israeli campaign into a global coalition.

However, internationalizing the war has faced diplomatic obstacles. The U.S. Navy's operational exhaustion has limited its ability to escort oil tankers, and the global economic impact of the Hormuz closure has increased pressure on Washington. Trump has tried to pressure NATO allies to commit assets, but European capitals remain reluctant, leaving the U.S. to manage the naval campaign.

---

## Sharing the Post-War Spoils: Concessions to Moscow and Beijing

To maintain the neutrality of Russia and China, Trump has offered significant post-war concessions:
*   **Russia**: The U.S. effectively lifted sanctions on Russian oil exports, allowing Vladimir Putin to reap $150 million daily to stabilize the Russian economy.
*   **China**: During U.S.-China talks in Paris, Washington guaranteed China's access to Gulf energy resources (China imports 90% of its oil through the Strait).

This arrangement leaves Europe as the primary loser. While the U.S. continues to fund Ukraine's war effort, it is funding Russia's economy through oil purchases, offering no share of post-war Gulf influence to its European allies.

---

## Conclusion: The Isolation of U.S. Maritime Operations

The absence of the Israeli Navy from the Strait of Hormuz and the collapse of trilateral alliances highlight the isolation of U.S. forces. By keeping its warships in home waters to avoid Houthi strikes, Israel has prioritized its own security, leaving the U.S. Navy to face Iranian anti-ship batteries alone.

As global powers like Russia and China secure economic concessions from Washington, the limits of the coalition are clear. The inability to form a unified naval front suggests that the U.S. will bear the financial and military costs of the Gulf campaign, with little support from its closest allies.
