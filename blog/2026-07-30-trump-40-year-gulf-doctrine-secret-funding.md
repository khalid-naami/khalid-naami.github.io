# Trump's Gulf Blueprint: Secret Funding & 1987 Plan

A critical strategic question surrounds the current Middle East war: how is it being funded? Neither the United States Congress nor the Israeli Knesset has authorized or allocated the massive budgets required to sustain this high-intensity escalation. The first week of U.S. operations cost $11 billion, while the daily closure of Israel's economy consumes $3.7 billion. The answer lies in off-budget funding provided by what Donald Trump terms the "Good Partners"—referring to specific Gulf monarchies. This financial pipeline is not an impromptu arrangement; it is the execution of a strategic blueprint for energy and alliance control that Trump has advocated for nearly 40 years.

## The Financial Mystery: War Outside the Budgets

The scale of the current mobilization is financially unprecedented for a localized campaign. The deployment of aircraft carriers, the continuous operations of Patriot and THAAD batteries, and the mobilization of 50,000 U.S. troops have generated costs that exceed standard peacetime operational budgets.

Under normal democratic processes, these expenditures require legislative authorization. However:
*   **The U.S. Congress** has not approved a supplemental war funding package for the campaign.
*   **The Israeli Knesset** has not amended its national budget to account for the $3.7 billion daily operational cost of the war.
*   **The Pentagon** has kept its primary defense appropriations separate from the campaign's cost tracking.

This suggests that the war is being financed through a transnational, off-budget pipeline that bypasses legislative oversight.

## The "Good Partners": Gulf Financing of the Conflict

The primary source of the war's funding is the financial reserves of specific Gulf monarchies, primarily the United Arab Emirates. Trump has referred to these nations as "Good Partners" who are willing to pay for U.S. military protection.

This financing model shifts the U.S. military from a state-funded defense force to a transnational security provider. The Gulf funding covers the operational costs of the U.S. Navy and Air Force in the region, while also underwriting the post-war subversion campaign planned by Mossad and AMAN to depose the Iranian regime over the course of a year.

## The Off-Budget Funding of Israel's Campaign

A parallel financial pipeline supports the Israeli defense budget. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich has managed to sustain the daily $3.7 billion cost of the war without raising domestic taxes or depleting the national treasury:
*   **Private transfers**: Funds are routed through offshore entities and private banking channels directly to the Israeli defense establishment.
*   **Bypassing the Knesset**: The allocations do not appear in the official state budget presented to the Knesset, preventing public debate on the cost of the war.
*   **U.S. coordination**: The funding allows Israel to maintain high-intensity operations and purchase U.S. munitions without waiting for Congressional aid approvals.

This off-budget model insulates both the U.S. and Israeli administrations from domestic political pressure, allowing them to extend the conflict indefinitely.

## Trump's 40-Year Obsession: The 1987 Blueprint

This transactional use of the U.S. military is not a sudden improvisation. It is the execution of a worldview that Donald Trump has promoted since the late 1980s. 

In September 1987, as a 41-year-old real estate developer, Trump spent nearly $100,000 to publish full-page open letters in the *New York Times*, the *Washington Post*, and the *Boston Globe*. His letters accused the U.S. government of making the country "respected globally as a laughingstock" (مخزية دولياً) by providing free military protection to wealthy allies:
*   **The Gulf Obsession**: Trump argued that the U.S. Navy should not protect oil tankers in the Persian Gulf for allies (such as Japan and Western Europe) who did not contribute a single dollar to the cost of the fleet.
*   **The Transactional Model**: He demanded that the U.S. charge its allies for the protection of their energy supply lines, asserting that military power should be used to extract economic concessions.

## The 1988 Mandate: Targeting Kharg Island

In 1988, during the final phases of the Iran-Iraq War, Trump refined his strategy regarding Iran. He wrote that if a single Iranian bullet struck a U.S. ship or oil platform, the U.S. response should be immediate and disproportionate: the complete destruction of Iran's oil export hub, Kharg Island.

He also ridiculed the Iranian Navy, describing its assets as "small speedboats with machine guns" that could not stand against a superpower. Today, in 2026, Trump is attempting to execute this exact 1988 plan, using the threat of destroying Kharg Island to force a total surrender of the Iranian regime.

## "Peace Through Brutal Force": The Strategy of Surrender

Trump's strategic doctrine is defined by the concept of "Peace through Brutal Force" (السلام عبر القوة الغاشمة). This approach rejects traditional diplomacy in favor of systematic intimidation:
*   **Ultimatums**: Issuing rigid, 48-hour deadlines to open the Strait of Hormuz.
*   **Infrastructure targeting**: Threatening to destroy the power grids and oil terminals of adversaries to force immediate compliance.
*   **No compromises**: Demanding the full surrender of the opponent's strategic programs rather than negotiating balanced agreements.

From Trump's perspective, this method represents the ultimate form of "deal-making," where the threat of total destruction replaces the need for diplomatic concession.

## The Blackmail of Brussels and NATO

The transactional model has also been applied to European allies. Trump has threatened to withhold U.S. military protection in the Gulf and leave Europe exposed to energy shortages if European capitals do not contribute financially to the campaign.

However, European leaders in Brussels have rejected the demands:
*   **Refusal to pay**: Brussels has refused to contribute a single euro to the off-budget war chest.
*   **Diplomatic independence**: Germany and other key European states have maintained backchannels to Tehran, warning that U.S. actions risk a global energy collapse.
*   **Strategic awareness**: Europe recognizes that paying U.S. military bills would establish a precedent of vassalage, turning NATO into a mercenary force controlled by the White House.

## Asymmetric Realities: Challenging the 1988 Blueprint

The primary flaw in Trump's 40-year-old blueprint is the evolution of asymmetric warfare. In 1988, Iran's navy was easily overwhelmed; in 2026, the IRGC has developed a distributed arsenal of precision cruise missiles, suicide drones, and mobile coastal batteries.

Iran's military command has indicated it is prepared to absorb U.S. strikes on Kharg Island and its power grid. By demonstrating the ability to target Gulf desalination plants and shut down the Strait of Hormuz, Iran has shown that it can impose costs that the global economy cannot survive, challenging the assumptions of the 1987 letters.

## Conclusion: The Limits of Transnational Blackmail

The current war is a test of Trump's lifelong theory of transactional military power. By funding the campaign through off-budget Gulf resources, he has bypassed Congress and the Knesset, attempting to execute the 1987 blueprint of "Peace through Brutal Force."

However, the resistance of European allies and the asymmetric capabilities of Iran have exposed the limits of this model. The U.S. can threaten the destruction of Kharg Island, but it cannot protect the global economy from the resulting oil shock. The 40-year-old theory of military blackmail is facing its ultimate operational test, and the cost of proving it right may destroy the very alliance network Trump sought to exploit.
