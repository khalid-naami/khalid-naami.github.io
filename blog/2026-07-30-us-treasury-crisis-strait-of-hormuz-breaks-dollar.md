# Fiscal Crisis: How Hormuz Broke the U.S. Dollar

The military confrontation in the Middle East has exposed a critical vulnerability in the U.S. financial system. The consolidated financial statements released by the U.S. Department of the Treasury for the fiscal year 2025 reveal a net deficit of $41 trillion, an amount that financial analysts describe as an "accounting hurricane." With the Strait of Hormuz closed and energy-driven inflation rising, the Federal Reserve is trapped in a debt-servicing cycle that threatens the global reserve status of the U.S. dollar.

## The Accounting Hurricane: Inside the Treasury's Balance Sheet

The U.S. Treasury's financial statements for the fiscal year 2025 present figures that challenge the long-term sustainability of the national debt:
*   **Total Assets**: $6.06 trillion.
*   **Total Liabilities**: $47 trillion.
*   **Net Deficit**: $41 trillion.

This $41 trillion deficit does not include off-balance-sheet unfunded liabilities for entitlement programs like Social Security and Medicare. According to a study published in *Fortune* by Steve Hanke and David Walker, these programs add between $50 and $70 trillion in unfunded commitments over the next 30 years. While a sovereign nation printing its own reserve currency cannot technically go bankrupt in its own denomination, its financial credibility is stretched to the breaking point.

---

## The Interest Trap: Service Costs Exceeding Defense

The immediate operational challenge is the rising cost of servicing the existing national debt. In the fiscal year 2025, the annual net interest expense on the U.S. debt reached $1.22 trillion:
*   **Defense comparison**: The interest bill now exceeds the entire national defense budget.
*   **Medicare comparison**: The cost of debt service has surpassed the annual expenditures of the Medicare program.
*   **War demands**: The war against Iran has already cost over $200 billion, including $11 billion in the first week of operations.

This high interest expense means that a significant portion of federal tax revenue is consumed by interest payments, reducing the resources available for domestic programs and military operations.

---

## The Fed's Dilemma: Hormuz Inflation vs. Debt Service

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has created a supply-side shock that complicates monetary policy. The halting of oil transit has driven up global energy prices, pushing the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation metric, the Core Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index, to 2.7% and rising.

Under normal conditions, the Fed would raise interest rates to combat inflation. However, with the national debt approaching $39 trillion, every basis point increase in interest rates raises the annual cost of servicing that debt by billions of dollars. The Fed is caught in a trap: it cannot lower rates due to energy-driven inflation, but it cannot maintain high rates without expanding the federal deficit.

---

## The Vicious Feedback Loop of War Financing

The intersection of energy markets, inflation, and interest rates has created a vicious feedback loop for U.S. public finance:
\[\text{Hormuz Closure} \rightarrow \text{High Energy Prices} \rightarrow \text{Domestic Inflation} \rightarrow \text{High Interest Rates}\]
The loop operates as follows:
1.  The blockade of the strait drives up energy prices.
2.  High energy prices prevent the Federal Reserve from cutting interest rates.
3.  High interest rates increase the servicing cost of the $39 trillion U.S. debt.
4.  High debt-servicing costs expand the federal deficit, requiring more borrowing.
5.  This additional borrowing must be executed at higher interest rates because the war continues.

There is no exit from this cycle as long as the shipping channels remain closed.

## The Japanese Flight: Repatriation of Treasury Assets

The U.S. debt market is further pressured by shifts among foreign holders, particularly Japan. Japanese life insurance companies and financial institutions hold approximately $5 trillion in foreign assets, mostly concentrated in U.S. Treasuries.

As the Bank of Japan (BOJ) tightens its monetary policy, the yield on the 10-year Japanese Government Bond (JGB) is approaching 2.2%. If Japanese institutions begin repatriating their capital to capture these higher domestic yields, the largest marginal buyer of U.S. debt will turn into a net seller. This shift occurs precisely when the U.S. Treasury needs to borrow an additional $200 billion to finance the Middle East war.

## The Petrodollar Fracture: Settling Transit in Yuan

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz is accelerating the detachment of the global energy market from the U.S. dollar. To bypass the SWIFT banking system and avoid U.S. sanctions, shipping companies are increasingly paying transit tariffs to the IRGC's toll collection stations in alternative currencies.

Vessels are using the Chinese Yuan (RMB) and the Euro to settle transit fees, bypassing the dollar entirely. This practical decoupling demonstrates that the global energy trade can operate without relying on the greenback, undermining the petrodollar system that has supported U.S. financial hegemony since 1974.

---

## The BRICS Alternative: Bypassing SWIFT in Real Time

This shift is supported by the growth of alternative financial systems within the BRICS bloc. In 2025, intra-BRICS trade reached $500 billion, with more than half ($300 billion) settled in local currencies:
*   **Ruble-Yuan transfers**: Bilateral trade between Russia and China is settled directly in local currencies, avoiding the SWIFT network.
*   **The War Comparison**: The $200 billion cost of the U.S. campaign against Iran represents nearly half of the entire intra-BRICS local currency trade volume.

The expansion of these local-currency networks reduces the global demand for U.S. dollars, making it more difficult for the U.S. to export its inflation and finance its deficits through foreign capital.

## Trump's Strategic Paradox: Winning is Losing

Trump's business-oriented approach to geopolitics has created an economic paradox. His administration has attempted to use military force to secure energy dominance and force concessions from adversaries.

However, the financial realities of the conflict have turned this strategy against itself. Whether the U.S. achieves its military objectives or not, the economic cost of the campaign has damaged the financial structures that support its power. If the U.S. "wins" by destroying Iranian infrastructure, it does so at the cost of a global energy shock and high interest rates. If it "loses" through a prolonged stalemate, its fiscal credibility declines. The war has become a conflict where military victory cannot offset financial loss.

---

## Conclusion: The Debt Shield Shattered

The fiscal statistics released by the Treasury and the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz point to a fundamental shift in the global financial order. The dollar is losing its connection to the energy trade, while the U.S. national debt has reached levels that limit the country's strategic options.

The U.S. government can continue to print its reserve currency to meet its obligations, but it cannot print the oil that is blocked behind the strait or force foreign investors to buy its debt at low yields. The debt shield that has allowed the U.S. to finance its regional wars without domestic sacrifice is fracturing, and the final price of the Middle East campaign will be paid in the currency of structural economic decline.
