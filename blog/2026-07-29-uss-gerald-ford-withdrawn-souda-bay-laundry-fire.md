# USS Gerald R. Ford Withdrawn: Fire Forces Souda Bay Transit

The U.S. Navy's lead supercarrier, the USS *Gerald R. Ford* (CVN-78), has officially departed the Middle East combat zone, transiting to Souda Bay in Crete for emergency damage assessment. This exit follows a severe internal fire that raged for over 30 hours before damage control teams could extinguish the blaze. To sustain air operations, the Pentagon has relocated the carrier's wing of F-35 and F-15 fighter jets to land bases in the Persian Gulf, exposing host installations to direct retaliatory strikes.

## The Exit: USS Gerald R. Ford Departs the Combat Zone

The departure of the $13 billion USS *Gerald R. Ford* represents a significant reduction in U.S. sea-based power projection in the region. The supercarrier has transited to the Greek naval base at Souda Bay to undergo comprehensive technical inspections.

While the base can provide initial repair support, military analysts suggest the extent of the damage may require the vessel to return to its home port in Virginia for dry-dock repairs. The exit of the carrier leaves U.S. forces dependent on land-based facilities in the Gulf to coordinate operations, altering the tactical balance of the air campaign.

---

## The 30-Hour Fire: Laundry Room Narrative vs. Reactor Risks

The official narrative released by the Navy and published by *The New York Times* claimed the fire originated in the carrier's main laundry room (غرفة الغسيل) due to a dryer vent malfunction. The report stated the fire spread through the central air ventilation ducts to adjacent storage compartments.

However, independent military sources suggest the damage was far more severe. The fact that the fire burned for 30 hours before containment indicates it penetrated deep interior sections, affecting advanced electronics and wiring close to the ship's two A1B nuclear reactors. The extended duration of the fire forced a shutdown of non-essential systems, compromising the Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System (EMALS).

---

## Grinding Schedule: The Toll of a 10-Month Sea Deployment

The technical failures on the *Gerald R. Ford* are partly due to operational over-extension. The carrier had been on continuous deployment for 10 months without scheduled maintenance cycles.

Its mission profile was characterized by rapid shifts:
*   **Red Sea Operations**: Executing strikes against coastal launch pads and escorting commercial shipping.
*   **Caribbean Deployment**: Transiting to the Caribbean as part of the U.S. naval blockade of Venezuela to pressure President Nicolás Maduro.
*   **Middle East Redeployment**: Returning to the Mediterranean and Red Sea to support the air campaign.

This grinding schedule exhausted the crew of 4,500 sailors and placed a heavy burden on the ship's computerized launch systems, leading to component wear and safety failures.

---

## Shifting the Air Wing: F-35s Move to Prince Sultan Airbase

With the carrier's catapult systems disabled and the ship heading to Crete, the Pentagon was forced to relocate the carrier's air wing. The F-35C Lightning II and F-15E Strike Eagle fighters stationed on the carrier were transferred to land bases.

The primary destination for these aircraft is Prince Sultan Airbase in Saudi Arabia. This relocation allows the U.S. to maintain strike readiness over Iranian airspace, but it changes the logistical footprint of the air campaign, shifting the origin of U.S. sorties from international waters to regional sovereign territory.

---

## Gulf Land Bases Exposed: Iranian Threat to Host Nations

The relocation of U.S. strike aircraft to land bases in the Gulf exposes the host nations to security risks. Iranian officials have warned that any regional state allowing its territory to be used as a staging ground for strikes on Iran will face direct retaliation.

Following the transfer of F-35s, the IRGC has increased rocket and drone strikes targeting Prince Sultan Airbase. This has forced Saudi authorities to issue alerts regarding local air defenses, showing how the carrier's exit has shifted the security burden onto regional partners.

---

## Coalition Strains: British Refusal and Carrier Standoff

The carrier's withdrawal occurred alongside diplomatic friction between Washington and London. The U.S. had requested that European allies deploy naval assets to the Red Sea to manage Houthi threats, allowing U.S. carriers (*Gerald R. Ford* and *Abraham Lincoln*) to concentrate on Iran.

However, UK Prime Minister Keir Starmer refused to deploy a British aircraft carrier during the active phase of the operations. Trump expressed frustration with this decision, rejecting Starmer's later offer to send two ships after the U.S. had already degraded local coastal batteries. This friction highlights the lack of coordination within the coalition, leaving the U.S. to manage the campaign with degraded naval assets.

---

## Conclusion: The Vulnerability of Land-Based Staging Networks

The withdrawal of the USS *Gerald R. Ford* to Souda Bay and the relocation of its aircraft to Prince Sultan Airbase show the limits of carrier-based operations under high operational strain. The combination of a 30-hour fire and deferred maintenance has disabled the Navy's advanced capital ship.

As the U.S. relies on land bases in the Gulf and long-range B-1B Lancer bomber runs from the UK, the strategic vulnerability of its operations is clear. The dependency on regional land bases exposes host nations to Iranian missile strikes, suggesting that the carrier's exit has limited U.S. options and raised the risks for its regional allies.
